What to Do When Your AC Freezes Up

It surprises people every summer: it's hot outside, the house is getting warmer, and there's ice on the air conditioner. A frozen AC is a common problem, and the first steps to fix it are simple. Here's what's going on and what to do.

First, turn the cooling off. If you see frost or ice on the copper line going into the outdoor unit, or on the indoor coil, switch the thermostat from cool to off. Then set the fan to on. That keeps air moving across the coil and helps the ice melt faster. Running the AC while it's frozen can damage the compressor, which is the most expensive part of the system.

Give it time to thaw. Depending on how much ice there is, it can take a few hours. Put a towel or pan near the indoor unit in case the drain pan overflows while it melts. Don't chip at the ice or try to speed it up with a hair dryer or heat gun. The coil fins are thin and easy to puncture.

Check the filter while you wait. The most common cause of a frozen AC is poor airflow, and the most common cause of poor airflow is a dirty filter. When not enough warm air moves across the coil, the coil gets too cold and the moisture on it turns to ice. If the filter is gray or clogged, replace it.

Look at your vents and returns. Closed vents in unused rooms, or furniture pushed up against a return grille, can also cut airflow. Open the vents and clear the returns. Closing vents to save money tends to backfire, since the system was sized to move air through all of them.

Turn it back on once the ice is gone. When the coil is fully thawed and dry, switch back to cooling and keep an eye on it for the rest of the day. If the house cools normally and no ice comes back, you've likely found the problem.

If it freezes again, call for service. Repeated freezing usually means something a homeowner can't fix. Low refrigerant is a common cause, and refrigerant doesn't get used up. If it's low, there's a leak that needs to be found and repaired, not just topped off. A failing blower motor, a dirty indoor coil, or a problem with the thermostat can also cause it.

Warm nights matter too. Running the AC on a cool night with the thermostat set very low can sometimes cause icing on older systems. If you only see ice after cool evenings, try raising the setting a few degrees at night.

A frozen AC is frustrating, but it's often caught early enough to avoid bigger damage. If the ice keeps coming back, give us a call and we'll track down the cause.
