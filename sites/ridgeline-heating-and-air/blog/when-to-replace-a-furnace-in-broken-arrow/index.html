When to Replace a Furnace in Broken Arrow

Most furnaces in Broken Arrow get a pretty easy life. Our winters have cold snaps, but they come and go, and a lot of systems run for years without much trouble. That makes it hard to know when an old furnace has crossed the line from "needs a repair" to "needs replacing." Here are the signs we look at.

Age is the first thing to check. Look for a sticker on the side of the unit or inside the panel door with a manufacture date. A furnace that has been running a long time isn't automatically done, but older units tend to need parts more often, and some of those parts get harder to find. If yours is getting up there and you're starting to call for repairs every winter, it's worth asking the question.

Repair bills that keep coming back are the next sign. One repair on an old furnace is normal. A new problem every season is different. A good rule of thumb: if a single repair would cost a big share of what a new system costs, and the furnace is already old, putting that money toward a replacement usually makes more sense.

Watch your gas bill. If your usage has crept up from one winter to the next and the weather hasn't been any colder, the furnace may be losing efficiency. Dirty burners, a worn blower motor, or leaky ductwork can all cause it, and some of that can be fixed. But if the system has been tuned up and the bills still climb, age is usually the reason.

Uneven heat is another clue. If some rooms stay cold while others get too warm, or the furnace runs and runs without ever reaching the temperature on the thermostat, something isn't right. Sometimes it's ductwork or a thermostat problem. Sometimes the furnace just can't keep up anymore.

Pay attention to noises and smells. Banging, squealing, or rattling when the furnace kicks on points to a mechanical problem. A burning dust smell for a few minutes at the start of the season is normal. A smell that sticks around is not. If you ever smell gas, leave the house and call the gas company first.

Finally, check the flame and the carbon monoxide detector. On many gas furnaces you can see the burner flame. It should be mostly blue. A lazy yellow flame can mean the burners aren't getting enough air. Make sure you have a working carbon monoxide detector near the bedrooms and test it every fall. An older furnace with a cracked heat exchanger can leak carbon monoxide, and that is a reason to stop using it right away.

None of these signs alone means you need a new furnace. Put a few together, though, and it's time for an honest look.
